Recommended Vet & Screening Timeline

This combines baseline feline wellness care with Maine Coon-specific attention to cardiac and orthopedic risk. Current feline life-stage guidance supports at least an annual full examination for every cat, and at least every six months once a cat reaches senior age.

8–16 weeksNew kitten

Establish veterinary care

  • Book a full physical examination within the first few days home, ideally as a condition of the purchase contract.
  • Begin the kitten vaccine series, parasite assessment and prevention, microchip discussion, and a growth and nutrition plan.
  • Collect the breeder's paperwork: pedigree, vaccination and deworming records, parents' health screening results, and DNA certificates.
  • Discuss timing for spay or neuter, plus carrier training, litter setup, and indoor enrichment.
4–12 monthsGrowing kitten

Complete core preventive care

  • Finish the vaccine series and schedule the first adult wellness visit around the first birthday.
  • Track weight, body condition score, muscle condition, mobility, dental development, and behavior at each visit.
  • Report any gait change, weakness, or difficulty jumping promptly rather than waiting for the next appointment.
  • Maine Coons mature slowly and often keep growing well past a year, so weight gain alone is not a concern if body condition stays lean.
1–3 yearsYoung adult

Annual wellness plus a cardiac conversation

  • Have a full examination at least once a year, including weight, heart and lung auscultation, oral exam, and mobility assessment.
  • Discuss a baseline echocardiogram with a veterinary cardiologist, particularly if there is a murmur, a family history of HCM, breeder recommendation, or any plan to breed.
  • Breeding cats are commonly screened by echocardiogram on a regular, often annual, basis alongside DNA testing and hip evaluation.
  • Note that a heart murmur can be absent in an affected cat and present in a healthy one, so auscultation alone is not a screen.
4–6 yearsAdult

Maintain care and reassess intervals

  • Continue at least annual wellness visits, updating parasite prevention, dental care, diet, and lifestyle plans.
  • Revisit cardiac screening timing with your veterinarian or cardiologist, since a normal earlier scan does not rule out later-onset disease.
  • Request an orthopedic assessment if activity drops, jumps or stairs are avoided, grooming declines, or hind-end stiffness appears.
  • Establish baseline bloodwork values while the cat is healthy so later changes are easier to interpret.
7–10 yearsMature adult

Increase monitoring and lab work

  • Plan wellness visits every six to twelve months; many practices move to twice-yearly evaluation at this stage.
  • Discuss blood pressure measurement, urinalysis, and bloodwork to track kidney function, glucose, thyroid status, and anemia indicators.
  • Reassess cardiac imaging based on age, exam findings, clinical signs, and pedigree history.
  • Watch for subtle arthritis signs, which in cats usually show as reduced jumping or altered grooming rather than obvious limping.
10+ yearsSenior

Minimum twice-yearly visits

  • Schedule a full examination at least every six months, more often for any diagnosed condition.
  • Use regular bloodwork, urinalysis, blood pressure checks, dental assessment, and pain and mobility review as directed.
  • Escalate promptly for appetite or weight change, increased thirst or urination, reduced mobility, breathing changes, or behavior shifts.
  • Adapt the home: lower-sided litter boxes, steps to favored perches, and warm accessible resting spots.
Monthly at-home check: note body weight or condition, appetite, drinking and litter box changes, resting breathing rate and effort, willingness to jump, grooming quality, and any new limp or weakness. A trend recorded over several months is far more useful to your veterinarian than a single observation. Counting resting respiratory rate while your cat sleeps is one of the most valuable things an owner can track for a breed predisposed to heart disease.

Priority Conditions

Highest priority

Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy (HCM)

HCM causes the heart muscle to thicken, reducing the heart's ability to fill and pump efficiently. It is the most significant inherited health concern in the breed, and affected cats often appear completely normal until the disease is advanced. A Maine Coon-specific MYBPC3 variant is well documented, but it is not the only cause, so a clear DNA result does not rule out disease.

Watch for

  • Rapid or labored breathing
  • Fainting or collapse
  • Reduced stamina or appetite
  • Sudden painful rear-leg weakness

Screening

  • Echocardiogram by a veterinary cardiologist
  • DNA testing as a risk tool, not a diagnosis
  • Repeat imaging over the cat's life
Orthopedic

Hip Dysplasia & Arthritis

Hip dysplasia is a malformed hip joint that allows abnormal laxity, leading to pain, reduced mobility, and arthritis over time. Large, heavy-boned breeds such as the Maine Coon are a higher-priority population for orthopedic evaluation. Cats mask pain well, so changes in behavior often appear long before any visible limp.

Watch for

  • Reluctance to jump or use stairs
  • Stiffness after resting
  • Altered gait or bunny-hopping
  • Discomfort when hips are handled

Screening & care

  • Orthopedic examination
  • Radiographs when indicated
  • Lean body condition
  • Accessible litter boxes and ramps
Inherited / recessive

Spinal Muscular Atrophy (SMA)

SMA is an inherited neuromuscular disorder affecting the motor neurons that supply the muscles of the trunk and limbs. Signs typically appear in young kittens as an unsteady gait and progressive muscle loss rather than as sudden adult illness. It is not painful and affected cats can live as indoor pets, but it is fully preventable through DNA testing of breeding cats.

Watch for

  • Wobbly or swaying gait in a kitten
  • Hind-limb weakness
  • Visible muscle wasting
  • Reduced ability to jump

Screening

  • DNA test on both parents
  • Never pair two carriers
  • Neurologic assessment if symptomatic
Inherited / recessive

Pyruvate Kinase Deficiency (PKDef)

PKDef is an inherited enzyme deficiency that shortens red blood cell survival and causes intermittent hemolytic anemia. Severity varies widely, and some affected cats show only occasional episodes. Carriers do not develop disease themselves but matter greatly in breeding decisions, since two carriers can produce affected kittens.

Watch for

  • Episodic weakness or lethargy
  • Pale gums
  • Reduced appetite or weight loss
  • Jaundice in severe cases

Screening

  • DNA test on both parents
  • Bloodwork if symptoms appear
  • Avoid carrier-to-carrier pairings

Responsible Breeder Checklist

Documented registered pedigree, multiple generations
HCM screening by echocardiogram, not DNA alone
DNA panel appropriate to the line, including SMA and PKDef
Hip evaluation practices and honest orthopedic history
Results disclosed for both parents, with dates
Written contract, health guarantee, and return policy
Evidence of socialization and veterinary care
No claim that a particular line needs no testing

A negative genetic test lowers risk only for the specific variant tested. It does not eliminate HCM, and it does not replace cardiac imaging or ongoing veterinary monitoring. Record what you are told on the breeder worksheet.

Seek urgent veterinary help

  • Open-mouth breathing, blue or gray gums, or rapidly worsening breathing effort
  • Collapse, fainting, extreme weakness, or sudden inability to use one or both rear legs
  • Crying out with severe pain, especially with cold or painful hind limbs
  • Repeated unproductive straining to urinate, which is a life-threatening emergency in males
  • A kitten with rapidly progressing weakness or inability to stand
